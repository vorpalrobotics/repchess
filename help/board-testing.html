Board Play Testing

Menu → Test → Chessboard opens the Opening Quiz -- the way to check whether your preparation actually holds up on a real board, separate from the VR memory-palace walkthrough.

How it works

The quiz plays the opponent's side and expects you to find your own prepared standard response at each step. A wrong or missing move counts as a miss; the session keeps a running hit/miss score as you go.

Setting up a session

Before starting, choose the number of questions, the coverage scope (a whole opening system or a single castle), a maximum depth, and which rooms to test: all of them, only the ones you've marked memorized, or only the ones due for review.

Quizzing what's due

"Only rooms due for review" turns the quiz into a review session. Instead of walking from the opening and hoping to reach something that needs attention, each question starts inside a room that's due, at one of its own doors, and carries on from there. The rooms you're most overdue on come first, so a session cut short has still covered what matters most.

You get one question per due room -- asking the same room twice in a sitting isn't a second review of it -- so running out of questions means the session is finished, not truncated. If nothing is due, it says so instead of starting an empty run. That's good news, not an error.

When you're stuck

A wrong move snaps the board back and asks again, so there's a "Give up on this move" button under the board. It shows you the move, counts it as a miss, and carries on down the line rather than ending the question -- the moves after the one you were stuck on are often ones you do know, and they're still worth testing. Giving up after already guessing wrong doesn't count twice; it's one failure either way.

When you're guessing

Getting a move right because you worked it out, or got lucky, isn't the same as knowing it. Press "Uncertain about next move" before you move, and the answer still counts as a hit but the room's next review is brought forward -- to roughly halfway through its current interval -- without costing it a rung on the ladder. You produced the move, so you keep the step; what you've reported is that the interval is too long for that room right now.

It can only ever bring a review closer, never push one out, and pressing it a second time cancels it. The score bar counts these separately, so "8 hits, 5 of them guesses" reads differently from "8 hits".

It feeds your review schedule

Missing a move shortens the review interval of the room that move belongs to, so the next thing you'd have relied on getting wrong comes round sooner. The "not the move" message names that room, and the summary lists every room the session moved, with its new due date and an undo -- knowing where a lapse lives is the actionable half, since that's the room to go and stand in. A room you haven't put on the schedule is still named, it just isn't moved. It only ever moves rooms down, never up. See Marking Rooms as Memorized.

After a session

The summary shows your score; you can test again with the same set of questions to see if you've improved, or draw a fresh set from the same scope.

Under the score, if the session changed anything on your review schedule, is the list of what it changed: each room by name, whether it dropped a rung or just had its next review pulled closer, and when it's now due. The schedule is the one thing a quiz leaves behind after you close it, so it says so plainly rather than moving in the background.

There's an Undo under that list, which puts every room in it back exactly as it was. Sometimes a bad run isn't about the repertoire -- you were tired, or distracted, or misread the board -- and the schedule shouldn't have to wear it. One button covers the whole session, because that's usually the unit you'd want back. The list stays on screen afterwards, struck through, so you can still see what the session did.

Two things it deliberately doesn't do. A room that something else changed in the meantime -- a grade you gave it in the VR walk in another tab -- is left alone and reported as skipped, since that newer judgement beats undoing an older one. And undoing doesn't put a room back in play for the rest of the session: a room you've already missed doesn't become fresh evidence again, so missing it a second time still won't move it.
